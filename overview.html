Monitoring harmful algal bloom

Harmful algal blooms (HABs) are phytoplankton blooms that lead to problems in the environment due to a variety of factors including fish kills and contamination of shellfish. Monitoring the taxonomy of phytoplankton and their abundance in the ecosystem is an important component of managing HABs. Metabarcoding is well suited for characterizing the taxonomy of phytoplankton communities. qPCR and dPCR are suitable for identifying the presence and abundance for HAB species when the target species have already been identified.

Relevant policies:

  • EBV:
    • Eco_EF_phe_ppla_FW
    • Eco_EF_phe_habi_MA
  • MSFD:
    • Descriptor 5: D5C3, D5C4, D5C6- Monitoring the impact of eutrophication on harmful algal species abundance

Recommended methods:

  • Metabarcoding for taxonomic characterization of the whole phytoplankton community
  • qPCR/dPCR for presence and abundance of specific target HAB species

Examples of application:

  • MARCO-BOLO D2.4 Junger et al. 2026 preprint
  • Gibbons et al. 2026

Detecting non-indiginous species

Non-indigenous species are species that are found outside their documented or historical range, often due to human activity. They do not need to be found in large amounts for their presence to potentially threaten regional biodiversity so monitoring their presence is an important component of determining ecosystem health. Metabarcoding is well suited for characterizing the taxonomy of organisms present, and may identify non-indigenous species that are present at low abundance. qPCR and dPCR can be used to identify and quantify the presence of targeted non-indigenous species.

Relevant policies:

  • MSFD:
    • Descriptor 2: D2C1, D2C2, D2C3- Measuring levels of non-indigenous fish species

Recommended methods:

  • Metabarcoding for taxonomic characterization of the whole community
  • qPCR/dPCR for presence and abundance of specific target non-indigenous species

Multi-species Composition

Utilized for broad biodiversity assessments across multiple trophic levels simultaneously using high-throughput sequencing platforms. Metabarcoding yields taxonomic composition of the community but does not typically provide absolute species abundance. It involves extracting the DNA from the environment and amplifying DNA regions targeting specific taxonomic groups using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) which creates copies of DNA sequence of the targeted region.

Pros:

  • Non-invasive and relatively inexpensive compared to traditional methods
  • Provides detailed taxonomic information about certain groups

Cons:

  • Incomplete reference database can limit the ability to identify organisms present
  • Generally does not provide absolute abundance data
    • But in certain circumstances absolute abundances can be derived when seawater sampling volumes and DNA yields are available (see preprint: Crédeville et al.

Quantifying Nucleic Acids

Best for targeted species monitoring (e.g., invasive pests or highly endangered target species) where quantitative sensitivity is paramount. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) and digital droplet PCR (dPCR) yields information on the presence and abundance of specific targeted taxa or species. qPCR involves measuring fluorescence signals after each cycle and comparing the fluorescence signal to a reference standard curve with dilutions of known quantity of DNA. dPCR separates the sample into many droplets and outputs how many droplets had positive reactions. Both methods involve extracting DNA from the environment and amplifying DNA regions of highly specified taxonomic groups using qPCR and dPCR machines. The fluorescent dye chosen can be general to all DNA present, or a fluorescent probe attached to a specific DNA sequence. The general dye is less expensive but less sensitive. It’s also possible to use multiple probes for up to 5 targets at time in multiplex PCR.

Pros:

  • Generates abundance and detailed taxonomic information of target organisms
  • Relatively quick and non-invasive compared to traditional microscopy or counting techniques

Cons:

  • Limited species specific primers already defined/li>
  • Incomplete reference databases to build appropriate primers

General resources:

Emerging Technologies

Metagenomics: Shotgun sequencing applications used to capture whole environmental genomes without targeted PCR bias. Metagenomics yields taxonomic identification and potential function of organisms present if performed at sufficient sequencing depth. It involves extracting DNA from the environment and sequencing the entire DNA without any specific amplification with primers. Metatranscriptomics: Metatranscriptomics yields taxonomic identification and functional potential of organisms present. Metatranscriptomics involves extracting RNA from the environment and sequencing the DNA without targeting with specific primers or amplifying targeted regions using primers.

Pros:

  • Both are PCR independent approaches which avoid PCR bias
  • Generates information on the taxonomy and the functional potential of organisms

Cons:

  • Analysis is computationally complex
  • Increased sequencing expenses due to the need for more sequences
  • Not ready to be implemented at large scale

DNA Extraction

DNA can be extracted using commercial kits or from personal lab protocols. Some testing may be required to optimize for organisms of choice, as different cell types may be more difficult to break apart for extraction. Following extraction, magnetic bead cleanups can ensure the quality of DNA is adequate. The whole process must be conducted in a lab space that is free from contamination, ideally in a PCR-hood to isolate quality yields. There are extraction kits that are specific for water analysis, but if benthic species are targeted or if particle attached organisms are of interest, DNA extraction kits designed for soil may be better suited. These kits typically account for possible PCR inhibitors present in higher abundance in particles or soil.

PCR Primers

PCR primers determine which sequences of DNA will be replicated during the PCR runs, and impact the sequencing platform used. In metabarcoding, primers are used to amplify a targeted region of DNA from taxonomic groups prior to sequencing, whereas qPCR/dPCR use target-specific primers to detect and quantify particular taxa. Primers for qPCR/dPCR must be carefully designed and thoroughly validated to ensure proper amplification. Blank sample controls and positive DNA controls are important to include in PCR runs.

Common metabarcoding targets:

  • 12S- Fish, mammal, vertebrate
  • 16S- Bacteria
  • 18S- Eukaryote
  • 28S- Eukaryote
  • COI- Metazoan
  • ITS- Fungi

Preparation for Sequencing

After PCR, DNA products must be cleaned and quantified. With metabarcoding, multiple samples are typically pooled together for sequencing, which requires an index to be added before pooling so later the sequences from each sample can be identified. The index used depends on the sequencing platform. After samples are indexed, the product must be cleaned before they are pooled together for sequencing. Extracted DNA must be properly cleaned, quantified and pooled with controls to prevent cross-contamination.

Sequencing Platforms

The number of sequences (sequencing depth) and type of sequences generated during sequencing runs are an important component of capturing community diversity. For metabarcoding, short reads using Illumina or Oxford Nanopore are standard but long reads using Oxford Nanopore or PacBio are becoming more common. Different sequencing platforms require different primer sets. Often pools of samples are shipped to core facilities for sequencing. For qPCR and dPCR, both technologies yield the abundance of target taxa but the process of obtaining the data is different.

Multi-species composition

Short reads: Illumina/Oxford Nanopore

  • Pros: Quick, less expensive, extensive support for sample analysis
  • Cons: Miss certain groups with large repeats

Long reads: Nanopore/PacBio

  • Pros: Better taxonomic resolution
  • Cons: Less resources for sample analysis since emerging technology

Denoising/Clustering

PCR creates many copies of the DNA sequences present, which means that many sequences could belong to the same organism. Grouping similar or identical sequences together is the next step in the data processing. Choosing how to resolve similar sequences impacts the comparability and replicability of studies. Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) and Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) are the most common ways to cluster sequences.

Metabarcoding:

Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs):

  • Pros: Grouping organisms by different taxa levels
  • Cons: Because sequences are grouped by relative similarity to each other, it can be difficult to compare the same dataset across studies
  • Tools: Swarm

Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs):

  • Pros: ASVs are resolved by single nucleotide differences, so they are comparable across studies
  • Cons: Potentially could capture within species variation
  • Tools: DADA2

Taxonomic Assignment

Reference databases are collections of taxonomically validated DNA sequences that can be used to identify the taxonomic origin of eDNA-derived sequences. Reference databases must be carefully curated, as an organism must be represented in a database in order to be identified in a sample. Many taxa-specific databases exist that are curated by experts in their fields. Biodiversity Metrics

Biodiversity can be assessed at different scales. Alpha diversity indices quantify species diversity and species evenness in one given area. Beta community variation compares community diversity between two areas. Multi-metric indices incorporate taxonomic species diversity and evenness and other metrics to one value for comparison.

Alpha diversity indices:

  • Species Richness
    • Counts the number of taxa observed. Not a robust measurement for eDNA due to influence by sequencing depth and not accounting for undetected taxa.
  • Shannon's Index
    • Assess richness and evenness and is less sensitive to rare species.
  • Simpson's and Inverse Simpson's Index
    • Counts the number of taxa observed. Not a robust measurement for eDNA due to influence by sequencing depth and not accounting for undetected taxa.
  • Chao1 Index
    • Not suitable for eDNA as it is influenced by sequencing depth, is heavily influenced by rare taxa and does not account for undetected taxa.

Beta community variation:

  • Bray-Curtis dissimilarity
    • Based on abundance of each taxa.
  • Jaccard distance
    • Based on presence/absence of each taxa.

Multi-metric indices:

  • Hill diversity
    • Combines Species Richness, Shannon’s diversity and Simpson’s evenness.
    • Must choose a q value.

Functional richness/diversity:

Taxa Abundance

After targeted PCR assays, nucleic acid copy abundance must be quantified to be placed in proper archives. Computer programs are available to process the data, but care must be taken for appropriate parameters. First, background fluorescent noise must be filtered from the fluorescent data. For qPCR, the quantification cycle of detection for each sample is determined and missing values are addressed. Then the fluorescence values are compared to the standard dilution curve to determine the amount of starting DNA template was present. For dPCR, since the sample is diluted to droplets of one nucleotide, sequence abundance is calculated by how many droplets had fluorescence signal amplification. A poisson correction is applied to account for droplets that may have more than one reaction. Proper documentation of data manipulation is essential for metadata. Final abundances must be placed into proper archives.

EVs (Essential Variables)

Essential Ocean Variables (EOVs) are defined by the Global Ocean Observing System (GOOS) to have priority measurements among national and regional monitoring systems. EOVs are the observations that can be integrated into monitoring programs to observe long-term change in ecosystems. Some of the Biology and Ecosystem (BioEco) EOVs are well suited for the application of eDNA. Essential Biological Variables (EBVs) are developed by the Group on Earth Observations Biodiversity Observation Network (GEO BON).

WFD (Water Framework Directive)

The Water Framework Directive (WFD) is a policy initiative by the European Commission to protect and restore water health. It describes monitoring requirements and criteria for assessing the health of water bodies. It requires assessing ecological status of water bodies through Biological Quality Elements (BQEs) to categorize ecosystems health into classes from bad to high.
