ddPCR / qPCR

Best for targeted species monitoring (e.g., invasive pests or highly endangered target species) where quantitative sensitivity is paramount. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) and digital droplet PCR (ddPCR) yields information on the presence and abundance of specific targeted taxa or species. It involves extracting DNA from the environment and amplifying DNA regions specific to highly specified taxonomic groups using qPCR and ddPCR machines. qPCR involves measuring fluorescence signals and comparing that to a reference standard curve. ddPCR separates the sample into many droplets and outputs how many droplets had positive reactions.

Pros:

  • Generates abundance and detailed taxonomic information of target organisms
  • Relatively quick and non-invasive compared to traditional microscopy or counting techniques

Cons:

  • Limited species specific primers already defined/li>
  • Incomplete reference databases to build appropriate primers

Sample collection

Sampling plans will vary depending on the target organisms and policy initiatives. Consistency in all steps of sample collection is necessary for reproducibility across studies. Quality metadata ensures that sampling efforts are properly documented and are necessary for submission into data archives.

Importance elements to consider are:

  • Depth of samples in water or soil
  • Volume of material processed
  • Processing time of material
  • Filter choice
  • Preservation of samples

Metadata should include at the very least:

  • Location (Latitude, Longitude) of sampling
  • Date and time of sampling
  • Depth of sampling
  • Volume of material processed
  • Environmental conditions (ie. temperature)

DNA Extraction

DNA can be extracted using commercial kits or from personal lab protocols. Some testing may be required to optimize for organisms of choice, as different cell types may be more difficult to break apart for extraction. Following extraction, magnetic bead cleanups can ensure the quality of DNA is adequate. The whole process must be conducted in a lab space that is free from contamination, ideally in a PCR-hood to isolate quality yields. There are extraction kits that are specific for water analysis, but if benthic species are targeted or if particle attached organisms are of interest, DNA extraction kits designed for soil may be better suited. These kits typically account for possible PCR inhibitors present in higher abundance in particles or soil.

PCR

PCR primers determine which sequences of DNA will be replicated during the PCR runs, and impact the sequencing platform used. Primers for metabarcoding target general groups of organisms while qPCR/dPCR use target primers for specific taxa groups or species. Primers for qPCR must be carefully selected and tested to ensure proper amplification. Blank sample controls and positive DNA controls are important to include in PCR runs.

Preparation for Sequencing

After PCR, DNA products must be cleaned and quantified. With metabarcoding, multiple samples are typically pooled together for sequencing, which requires an index to be added before pooling so later the sequences from each sample can be identified. The index used depends on the sequencing platform. After samples are indexed, the product must be cleaned before they are pooled together for sequencing. Extracted DNA must be properly cleaned, quantified and pooled with controls to prevent cross-contamination.

Sequencing Platforms

The number of sequences (sequencing depth) and type of sequences generated during sequencing runs are an important component of capturing community diversity. For metabarcoding, short reads using Illumina are standard but long reads using Oxford Nanopore or PacBio are becoming more common. Different sequencing platforms require different primer sets. Often pools of samples are shipped to core facilities for sequencing. For qPCR and dPCR, both technologies yield the abundance of target taxa but the process of obtaining the data is different.

Targeted sequencing

qPCR:

  • Cheaper but need a reference standard curve to establish abundance

ddPCR:

  • More expensive but absolute abundance achieved without reference curve

Submit raw sequences

After the sequences are run, pooled samples must be identified by their sequence index and sorted to separate the sequences from different samples, this process is called demultiplexing. Sometimes it is completed by the sequencing facility before the data is returned. The DNA sequences that are separated by sample must be placed into sequence archives with proper metadata to ensure that data generated is FAIR. Archive websites have detailed instructions and help desks to assist with difficulties.

Metabarcoding:

Quality check

Sequence errors are a natural result of PCR. General quality of sequences and confidence in base pair identities must be checked to increase data quality. Tools can be used to filter sequences at quality thresholds. Further identification and removing sequencing errors can be accomplished using specialized algorithms.

  • USEARCH

Taxonomic assignment

Reference databases are collections of identified gene sequences. Generated eDNA sequences are compared to a reference database for identifying the taxonomy of the sequence. Reference databases must be chosen carefully as an organism must be represented in a database in order to be identified in a sample. Many taxonomic group specific databases exist that are curated by experts in their fields. Regional reference indices may be used for sequence assignment validation.

Recommended reference databases:

  • Microzooplankton COI: Bucklin et al. 2021
  • National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI): NCBI
  • Barcode of life data systems (BOLD): BOLD)
  • Multimaker MetaZooGene: O'Brien et al. 2024

Biodiversity Metrics

Biodiversity can be assessed at different scales. Alpha diversity indices quantify species diversity and species evenness in one given area. Beta community variation compares community diversity between two areas. Multi-metric indices incorporate taxonomic species diversity and evenness and other metrics to one value for comparison.

Alpha diversity indices:

  • Species Richness
  • Shannon's Index
  • Simpson's and Inverse Simpson's Index

Beta community variation:

  • Bray-Curtis dissimilarity
  • Jaccard distance

Multi-metric indices:

  • Hill diversity

EOVs (Essential Ocean Variables)

Essential Ocean Variables (EOVs) are defined by the Global Ocean Observing System (GOOS) to have priority measurements among national and regional monitoring systems. EOVs are the observations that can be integrated into monitoring programs to observe long-term change in ecosystems. Some of the Biology and Ecosystem (BioEco) EOVs are well suited for the application of eDNA.

WFD (Water Framework Directive)

The Water Framework Directive (WFD) is a policy initiative by the European Commission to protect and restore water health. It describes monitoring requirements and criteria for assessing the health of water bodies. It requires assessing ecological status of water bodies through Biological Quality Elements (BQEs) to categorize ecosystems health into classes from bad to high.

Potential integration:

  • Benthic invertebrates taxonomy and abundance
  • Fish taxonomy and abundance
